2. Disposition stocks: one auction every two minutes

The market-structure observation that opens the episode: matching for Taiwan disposition stocks has moved to a periodic call auction every two minutes (from continuous trading). The host's read is that this turns the tape into "everyone gaming each other, with fake orders showing up more often."

✅ The rule description is accurate. Disposition stocks do move to periodic call auction during the disposition window; his account of the mechanics is correct.
❓ But "more fake orders, everyone gaming each other" cannot be verified. This is a feel-of-the-tape description with no public data to check it against (you would need order-level detail and cancellation-rate statistics). The direction is plausible — indicative auction prices are easier to push with size, and cancelling is cheap, so the incentive structure genuinely does reward spoofing. But "plausible" and "happened" are different things, and the episode offers no data.
💡 Practical takeaway for retail: during a disposition window, the order book you see is worth less than usual. With matching only every two minutes, resting orders are closer to signalling than to real supply and demand. Read this alongside the MediaTek disposition segment in EP685.

Group takeaways

Revenue across the chain is hard data, and it is uniformly strong. 15 of 16 names posted positive monthly revenue YoY, ranging from +18.6% to +181.8%; the only negative is Chicony at −12.2%. On revenue alone, there is no sign of this chain weakening.

But prices are down across the board, and the size of the drawdown has almost nothing to do with revenue strength. Sitting 15–30% below the three-month high is the norm; only TSMC (−4.7%) and AVC (−2.2%) held. The fastest grower, Alchip (+181.8%), is down 28.1%; Accton (+71.7%) is down 21.6%; while the shallowest drawdown belongs to TSMC, whose revenue growth is "only" +44.7%. What the market is pricing right now is safety, not growth rate.

Valuation dispersion is what this table is really about. The three substrate names (Nan Ya PCB 148.7x, Kinsus 159.2x, Unimicron 66.6x) and optics (LandMark 234.5x, HLJ 101.5x) are in pure narrative-premium territory; LandMark's 234.5x needs its +176.5% revenue growth to persist for several more quarters just to stand still. At the other end, Auras at 24.2x against +116.7% growth and Lotes at 28.2x are the two most reasonable on the multiple — but Lotes' revenue growth has decelerated visibly to +18.6%, so cheap is cheap for a reason.

⚠️ Chicony is the one to flag in red: an 11.8x trailing PE looks like the cheapest thing on the table, but it is the only name with negative revenue YoY (−12.2%). That combination is the textbook value-trap signal, not a "low valuation opportunity" — the multiple is low because the denominator (past earnings) has not caught up with the decline, not because the market got it wrong.

At that point the thing to revisit is the still-cheap core leader (TSMC at 32.5x) rather than the secondary suppliers that already ran. Which also explains why the host nods along to the listener's "keep the core intact" allocation.

9. Assessment (invest-playbook frame)

What holds up

The Google segment has a complete argument, and its conclusion matches the valuation picture. The "outsource the R&D risk, collect the rent" frame lines up with Berkshire's three rounds of buying, and GOOG's forward PE of 25.34x sits at a 3.2% valuation percentile — hot narrative with a floor-level multiple is an unusual combination. The host also states the downside himself ("if the market decides Google lost its frontier-AI call option, the dream multiple gets cut"), and that two-sided presentation is worth more than one-way bullishness.

Every revenue figure for the Taiwan AI infra names checks out. All six endorsed names verify: TSMC +44.7%, Delta +47.7%, Accton +71.7%, Bizlink +59.6%, Auras +116.7%, AVC +57.4%. The fundamental base under "keep the core intact" is real.

"Already out of old AI" is a position call with content. Exiting while Alchip runs +181.8% and GUC +158.4% on revenue — and both share prices lag (−28.1% / −13.8%) — is more mature than the linear "revenue grows so I'm bullish" inference.

What to discount

"Apple is most conservative on AI yet best YTD" is a selectively chosen baseline. It holds only within the GOOG/MSFT/META subset; add NVDA (+16.8%) and AMZN (+18.0%) and it fails. Apple's 33.25x forward PE is also the most expensive of the three, and the mean analyst target of $340.98 is barely above the current price. The data does not support "skipping capex won."

The DeepMind exodus could not be independently confirmed here. Neither Hassabis stepping down nor Jeff Dean founding Discovery Loop yielded a citable official announcement or tier-one media confirmation during verification. The first link in the chain is the weakest, and the other two are built on top of it.

Two homophone errors in the transcript would mislead anyone reading it literally. "Apple's new CEO" is actually Berkshire's new CEO Greg Abel; the garbled thermal reference is Auras/AVC. Neither damages the argument, but the lesson stands: when researching from ASR transcripts, names and tickers must be verified separately.

"More fake orders in disposition stocks" and "Meta wants to be a landlord" both lack numbers. The first is feel-of-the-tape with no public data to check; the second is inference with no management statement or financial line behind it. Both are plausible in direction, and neither should be treated as fact.

Risks and the other side

The biggest risk is reading "strong revenue + price drawdown" straight through as an entry point. Revenue really is strong across the chain, but the reason prices fell may not be that the market is wrong — it may be that the market is pricing the second derivative of growth ahead of time. Delta's trailing PE is already 56.97x and Bizlink's 43.5x; if 800V power or CPO optics volume slips out in time, multiple compression does not need a downturn to show up — "growth slows" is enough. This is the same conclusion as EP686.

Second, Google's "digital landlord" frame is a double-edged sword, not a straightforward bull case. Rent means steady cash flow without paying for the frontier-model arms race; the price is losing the premium that comes with technological leadership. If the market one day decides landlords deserve utility multiples, 25.34x forward will not look cheap. A 3.2% valuation percentile can be read as "cheap" — or as "the market has already begun cutting its growth expectations." The episode only offers the first reading.

Third, the financial content here is too thin to decide anything on. Ticker density is the lowest in recent episodes, all six Taiwan names are agreement-with-a-listener rather than active recommendations, and two of the three US names (Apple, Meta) have visible flaws in their arguments. Read this episode as the carrier for one idea — Google's changing role — and it works; read it as a watchlist and it will disappoint.

Credibility verdict

On verifiable financial and market data the episode largely holds up: all six Taiwan revenue figures check out, Berkshire's buying is right at the order-of-magnitude level, and the disposition-stock mechanics are described accurately. The problems cluster in two places: a selectively set comparison baseline in the narrative layer (Apple YTD), and a missing independent source for the first link in the argument chain (the DeepMind exodus).

Overall: directionally credible, but verify the multiples and the comparison set yourself. What is worth keeping is the "Google from researcher to landlord" frame — it offers a new angle on the AI giants and, unusually, comes with its own downside attached. As for the six Taiwan names, the episode adds no information; the numbers filled in here are the actionable part: revenue strong across the board, prices down across the board, valuations severely dispersed — and the cheapest name on the table (Chicony at 11.8x) is the one value trap with negative revenue growth.
