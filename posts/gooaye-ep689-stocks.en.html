The Micron dividend dispute and DRAM stacking/DPACK technical details have no cross-checkable source and are left unresolved.

Risks and the other side

The first risk is treating "the component trade is done" as a confirmed signal. MU is still about 25% below its high, not fully caught up; rotating early into the bottleneck trade before memory's own rally has finished could miss both. This framework describes relative sequencing, not a green light to rotate blindly into bottleneck names the moment component looks "done."

The second risk is how much valuation percentiles vary within the same sector, which "memory is cheap" as a blanket statement obscures. STX sits at a 72.8% valuation percentile, nowhere near MU's 19.2% or WDC's 12.0% — buying STX on the general "memory is cheap" narrative would mean overpaying.

Third, the Value-add trade stop is entirely hypothetical for now — the episode gives no matching name or trigger condition, just the third step of a logical chain. If capital keeps flowing into component and bottleneck, this stop may never become urgent, so it's not a reason to position early.

Credibility verdict

This episode's core framework has a fairly high verification rate: the capital-rotation logic, the MU/AMKR/Nanya Tech/ASE price checks, and the WSJ off-balance-sheet report all hold up under cross-checking — far more substantial than a narrative simply asserted.

The problems cluster around specific operational details and vague, unnamed comments: the bottleneck trade's specific tactics have no primary source; "AI giants' growth slipped" names no one but could easily be misread as NVIDIA; "three floors" is a personal number that's easy to mistake for a market-wide event.

STX and MU are both "memory/storage," yet sit at wildly different valuation percentiles — that's the clearest example of why.
