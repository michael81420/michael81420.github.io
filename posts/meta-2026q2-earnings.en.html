Meta．Q2 2026 Earnings, Unpacked ｜🧾

Revenue of $60.80B, +28% YoY — one of the strongest prints in eight quarters. Yet EPS came in at $6.18 against a $7.22 consensus: a 14.4% miss that ends a six-quarter beat streak. The stock fell 8% that day.
The real story isn't EPS: free cash flow collapsed to $1.75B, down 86.8% QoQ. Built from the IR release, call summaries, and Futu data. Not investment advice.
Source: Meta IR release + earnings call summaries Period: 2026-04-01 – 2026-06-30 Reported: 2026-07-29 after close Compiled: 2026-08-03

TL;DR · What actually mattered this quarter

💧 Free cash flow went to almost nothing — this matters far more than the EPS miss. Operating cash flow was $31.86B (+24.7% YoY — the core business is fine), but quarterly capex of $31.08B (+57% QoQ, +82% YoY) ate nearly all of it, leaving FCF of just $1.746B — down 86.8% QoQ and 80.6% YoY, the lowest in eight quarters. No one-time charge explains this. It is the first clear sign that the AI build-out is structurally narrowing cash flow.

📉 First EPS miss in six quarters — and it still looks bad after stripping the one-offs. $6.18 vs $7.22 expected. The quarter carried $2.4B of legal costs plus $1.18B of severance ($3.58B combined); adding those back lifts operating margin to roughly 36.8%. But a year ago it was 43% and last quarter 40.6%. Even with every one-off removed, margin is still 6.2pp lower — depreciation and headcount cost are eroding it structurally, not as noise.

📊 Ad volume is decelerating; price is carrying the load. Impression growth fell for a second straight quarter (+19% → +14%), price per ad held flat at +12%, and Family daily actives decelerated for a third straight quarter (+8% → +4% → +3%). Revenue growth of +28% holds for now, but the burden is shifting from volume to price. If impressions break below +10% next quarter, price has to carry an even larger share.

🛑 Buybacks to zero, debt up sharply — financial priorities have been rewritten. Share repurchases this quarter: $0 (previously billions per quarter). Long-term borrowings jumped from $58.75B to $83.66B (+42.4% QoQ). Everything goes to capex. This is the same phenomenon as Alphabet issuing both equity and debt this quarter — the large platforms are collectively switching from returning cash to asking for it.

★ On valuation: the post-earnings drop pushed forward PE to 17.9x, the 5.2 percentile of its one-year range — among the lowest in its peer group. But be precise about what that means: this cheapness was created by the sell-off, not by long-term undervaluation. The reason it is cheap is precisely the capex and FCF doubts — and those doubts don't get answered until next quarter.

1. Start with cash flow: the core business is fine, capex ate it

This is the single most important chart of the quarter. Meta's core business is not broken: operating cash flow went $25.56B → $32.23B → $31.86B, up 24.7% in a year. The advertising machine still prints. What broke is the rate at which cash goes back out: capex went $16.54B → ~$19.0B → $30.1B, up 57% in a single quarter.

Subtract one from the other and you get free cash flow: $9.02B → $13.23B → $1.746B. The lowest in eight quarters; the second-lowest was $9.02B a year ago — meaning this quarter came in at one-fifth of the second-worst print.

How is the shortfall covered? Meta halted share buybacks entirely ($0) and raised long-term borrowings from $58.75B to $83.66B — $24.9B of new debt in one quarter. Cash and equivalents fell from $23.43B to $15.46B.

📌 Read the three together: buybacks to zero, heavy new borrowing, FCF down to a rounding error. These aren't three separate data points — they're three faces of one fact: Meta's cash generation can no longer keep pace with what it wants to build. Management makes no attempt to hide this; the FY26 capex guide still tightened upward. It's a deliberate choice, not a loss of control. But deliberate choices carry costs, and the cost here is depreciation for years to come.

★ A data discrepancy worth flagging: the IR release puts capex at $31.08B, while Futu's cash flow statement shows net PP&E activity of $30.12B. The chart uses the latter (it reconciles with FCF); prose citing the IR figure uses $31.08B.

2. Unpacking the EPS miss: one-offs explain only half of it

Two one-time items hit the quarter: $2.4B of legal costs and $1.18B of severance (roughly 8,000 roles cut in May 2026), $3.58B combined. Add them back and operating margin recovers from the reported 31% to about 36.8%.

Most commentary stops there and concludes "it's fine once you strip the one-offs." That conclusion doesn't hold: a year ago the figure was 43%, last quarter 40.6%. 36.8% is still 6.2pp below last year and 3.8pp below last quarter. Of the 12pp total decline, one-offs explain 5.8pp — the remaining 6.2pp is structural.

Where does the structural piece come from? Management listed four drivers on the call: employee compensation, infrastructure costs (depreciation), legal-related costs, and third-party AI token costs. That last one is a new expense category — meaning the inference cost of Meta's own AI products is now large enough to call out separately.

3. Advertising: volume slowing, price holding the line

Ad revenue was $59.36B, +27% YoY, or 97.6% of total revenue. Strong on the surface — but split it into volume and price and you can see the centre of gravity moving:

① Impressions fell from +19% to +14% YoY, a second consecutive deceleration. ② Price per ad held flat at +12% — it neither picked up the slack nor deteriorated. ③ Family daily actives went +8% → +4% → +3%, three straight quarters of deceleration, at an absolute level of 3.60 billion people.

User-side saturation is expected — Meta already reaches most of the connected world. But the impression slowdown deserves more attention than the DAP slowdown: impressions depend not only on how many people there are but on how many ads each person sees, which is a variable Meta still controls. Its decline suggests ad-load expansion is running out of room too.

📌 A falsifiable test: if impression growth breaks below +10% next quarter without a matching lift in price per ad, ad revenue growth (currently +27%) will show it directly. Of the three numbers, this is the one that breaks first.

4. Reality Labs: bigger losses, but the money moved

Revenue of $431M (+16% YoY) against an operating loss of $4.62B, wider than last quarter's $4.03B. Cumulative losses since the segment was established in 2020 are roughly $88B.

The call revealed a shift in framing: about 70% of Reality Labs spend has moved from VR headsets to AI glasses and wearables. That's a clear strategic pivot — the loss didn't shrink, but the money is pointed somewhere else. There is still no disclosure of AI glasses unit volumes or revenue split, which is the first concrete number to watch next quarter.

One more thing said only on the call and not in the release: management proactively flagged that several youth-related trials in the US are scheduled this year, and stated plainly that they could result in material loss, outside the $2.4B of legal costs already accrued. When management pre-announces litigation risk unprompted, it usually means they assess the probability as non-trivial.

5. Guidance: tightening upward for a second quarter, with no sign of stopping

Two levels to this. First: next-quarter revenue guidance of $61–64B against this quarter's actual $60.80B implies continued sequential growth at the midpoint — management has confidence in core momentum.

Second, and more important: the FY26 capex trajectory has gone $120-135B (Q4'25 call) → $125-145B (Q1'26) → $130-145B (this quarter). Each time, the low end moves up and the high end stays put. That "tightening upward" pattern means management is increasingly certain they will spend a lot, and still unsure whether they'll spend to the ceiling. Three consecutive moves in the same direction, with no topping signal.

Total expense guidance tightened the same way, from $162–169B to $165–169B (+$3B at the low end). Costs and capex moving up together is the only consistent direction in this quarter's guidance.

6. Valuation: the cheapness was created by the sell-off

📉 Forward PE 17.9x, 5.2 percentile of its one-year range (one-year mean 26.2x); forward PS 5.1x at the 0.8 percentile. Both near one-year lows.

But be clear on how that happened: the stock went $585.61 → $539.03 (−8%) the day after earnings, then recovered partway to $556.71 over three sessions. The price fell first, and that's what produced the multiple — and it fell precisely because of the capex, FCF and margin concerns above.

So "cheap" and "mispriced" are not the same claim here. To call it mispriced you first need next quarter's FCF to recover and margin to stabilise. The doubts haven't been falsified — they've only been priced.

Analyst consensus (updated 2026-08-02, one day post-earnings): 42 analysts, 88.1% buy / 11.9% hold / 0% sell, average target $763.61 (high $1000 / low $595), implying roughly +37% against $556.71. But this is one day of post-earnings updates; target-price cuts typically lag results by one to two weeks, so that +37% shouldn't be treated as a conclusion yet.

Cycle intensity (invest-playbook): digital advertising is a moderate-cycle business — tied to macro ad budgets, though Meta's share is stable. The real cyclical variable is the capex cycle, clearly in its expansion phase. Expansion phases defer depreciation onto the income statement over the following two to three years — a cost that forward PE rarely captures in full.

7. Peer group scan

Group definition: digital advertising platforms. 97.6% of Meta's revenue comes from targeted advertising, putting it alongside Google search ads, Amazon retail media, Snap/Pinterest/Reddit social advertising and The Trade Desk's programmatic platform — all driven by macro conditions and advertiser budgets. (Meta is also a major AI capex spender, but that's a different theme; this table focuses on the advertising business.)

One cross-sectional observation: META 5.2%, GOOGL 3.2%, AMZN 0.8%, TTD 3.6% — all four sit in single-digit valuation percentiles. That isn't a single-company problem; it's a group-wide compression, which looks more like a flow-of-funds effect than a fundamental one.

PINS is the clear exception: forward PE 38.3x at the 98.8 percentile, near its historical high, a categorically different situation that shouldn't be grouped into the same "cheap" frame. SNAP's forward PE is negative on losses, which makes its 73.6 percentile unreadable as a valuation signal.

9. Both sides of the argument

Three reasons to be constructive:

① The advertising business is genuinely strong. Revenue +28% YoY is among the best of the last eight quarters, price per ad is steady at +12%, and impressions — while decelerating — are still growing. Operating cash flow +24.7% YoY shows no erosion in the cash engine.

② Valuation is near the bottom of the peer group. Forward PE 17.9x at the 5.2 percentile, with 88.1% buy ratings and a target implying +37%. If the capex doubts are falsified next quarter, the re-rating room is real.

③ Capex is investment, not bleeding. The money is going into infrastructure that can run AI. If it converts into recommendation-engine efficiency and Meta AI monetisation, this is the trough of an investment cycle rather than the start of a decline.

Three reasons to be cautious:

① $1.746B of FCF is not a one-off. FY26 capex guidance of $130–145B implies more spending in coming quarters, so cash-flow pressure most likely persists rather than reverses. Buybacks are already halted and debt rose $24.9B in a quarter — financial flexibility is being consumed.

② The margin decline survives the add-back. 36.8% vs 43% a year ago, a 6.2pp gap. Depreciation and AI token costs are both rising line items; neither reverses on its own.

③ The volume/price structure of advertising is deteriorating. Impressions and DAP are decelerating together while price carries the load; litigation risk remains unquantified and could produce losses beyond the $2.4B accrued.

★ There is really only one point of disagreement: how many years you think this AI capex cycle takes to pay back. The bulls don't deny FCF looks bad; the bears don't deny the ad business is still growing. The difference is whether you're willing to wait out the depreciation.
