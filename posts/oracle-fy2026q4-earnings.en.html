Oracle．FY2026 Q4 Earnings, Unpacked ｜🧾

Revenue and EPS both beat, RPO exploded to $638B (+363% YoY), and OCI grew 93% — the fastest acceleration among the major clouds. Then the stock fell 8.5% the next day and 35% over the following seven weeks.
What the market rejected wasn't demand. It was where the money comes from and how much it dilutes. Built from the IR release, the call transcript and Futu data. Not investment advice.
Source: Oracle IR release + earnings call transcript Period: 2026-03-01 – 2026-05-31 (FY26 Q4) Reported: 2026-06-10 Compiled: 2026-08-03

⚠️ First, the quarter mapping: Oracle has no "calendar Q2 2026" report

Oracle's fiscal year ends in late May (FY Q1 = Jun–Aug, Q2 = Sep–Nov, Q3 = Dec–Feb, Q4 = Mar–May). As of 2026-08-03, the most recent reported quarter is FY2026 Q4, covering 2026/3/1–5/31, which straddles the end of calendar Q1 and the start of calendar Q2.

No Oracle filing maps cleanly onto calendar Q2 2026 — the quarter that actually covers June–August is FY2027 Q1, due in mid-September 2026. This note analyses the most recent available report, and the title and badges use Oracle's own fiscal naming rather than relabelling it "Q2."

TL;DR · What actually mattered this quarter

🧾 On the demand side this is the strongest of the major clouds, and that isn't in dispute. RPO — contracted but unrecognised revenue — grew another $85B in the quarter to $638B, up 363% YoY. OCI revenue was $5.79B, +93% YoY, against AWS at +37%, Azure at 43% and Google Cloud at +82%: the lowest base but the steepest acceleration. Four customers each signed contracts above $8B in Q4, and GPU utilisation ran at 97.5%.

💸 But the money doesn't add up — and it's the second straight year of overspending. FY26 capex came in at $55.7B against the company's own mid-year guide of $50B. Free cash flow was −$23.7B (FY25: just −$0.4B). Interest-bearing debt went from $92.6B to $129.5B in a year. FY27 requires roughly $70B more of net cash spending, and the company announced $40B of refinancing, $20B of it equity issuance — straight dilution.

📉 Gross margin has fallen for eight straight quarters, and management says it will fall further. TTM gross margin went 71.3% → 65.8%. CFO Hilary Maxson said plainly on the call that margin had "stepped down about 5 percentage points, in line with expectations," and conceded FY2027 will see further decline due to data-centre ramp timing and product mix. This is a deliberate trade of margin for OCI capacity speed, not an accident.

⏳ How much of that $638B is near-term? Only 12%. Disclosed on the call: just 12% converts to revenue within 12 months, 34% falls in months 13–36, and the remaining 54% is beyond three years. The backlog is thick, but the conversion window is long — far weaker support for near-term valuation than the headline suggests.

★ Unlike the common "numbers were fine but it fell, so it's a flow-of-funds story" reading: Oracle has specific, company-level cracks here — capex above its own guide, FCF negative, dilutive equity issuance announced, and shareholder law firms already investigating the capex disclosures. Those explain −35% on their own, without invoking a sector-wide move — though the July–August AI deleveraging environment did compound it.

1. $638B of RPO is thick, but it converts slowly

The RPO line itself is not the problem: $455B → $523B → $553B → $638B, up $85B in one quarter and 363% YoY. These are signed contracts, not management projections. In Q4 alone, four customers each committed above $8B.

The issue is conversion pace. The split disclosed on the call (absent from the release): 12% recognised within 12 months, 34% in months 13–36. That works out to roughly $77B becoming revenue over the next year. Against the FY27 revenue target of $90B, RPO clearly provides a solid floor — but the $638B headline has less to do with next year's income statement than it intuitively appears to.

📌 A fuller backlog means higher execution risk. Q1 FY27 revenue guidance was raised sharply from this quarter's actual +21% to +27–29%, and RPO conversion is the basis for that confidence. But it also means Oracle has packed its "signed but undelivered" pipeline tight — and whether the data centres get built on schedule becomes a new source of risk. FY26 delivered over 1.2GW of capacity; Q1 FY27 needs to deliver close to another 1GW.

2. The chart that actually broke the stock: not enough money

Operating cash flow is actually good: $32.0B, +54% YoY. The core business generates more cash than it used to. But FY26 capex of $55.7B exceeded it by $23.7B — and that gap is the negative free cash flow.

More consequentially: $55.7B overshot the company's own $50B guide. That is two consecutive years of capex overruns. The market can accept "spending a lot"; it accepts "spending more than you said you would" much less readily — the latter damages the credibility of capital discipline itself.

The gap is covered by borrowing: interest-bearing debt rose from $92.6B at FY25 year-end to $129.5B at FY26 year-end, $37B in a single year. FY27 requires $40B more of refinancing, $20B of it via an ATM equity programme — direct per-share dilution, not just interest expense. Shareholder law firms are already investigating whether the company's capex disclosures misled investors.

Oracle does have a buffer its peers lack: on large AI contracts, the portion where customers prepay or bring their own GPUs (BYOH) now totals $75B cumulatively, which management stresses materially reduces what Oracle must finance itself. That's genuine differentiation — but evidently not enough, or the $40B raise wouldn't be needed.

3. Gross margin: eight straight quarters down, with no floor yet

TTM gross margin fell from 71.3% in FY25 Q1 to 65.8% this quarter, a cumulative 5.5pp. The last four quarters read 69.7% → 68.5% → 67.1% → 65.8% — roughly 1.3pp per quarter, strikingly regular.

To management's credit, they didn't dodge it. CFO Hilary Maxson volunteered that gross margin had "stepped down about 5 percentage points, in line with expectations," and offered a recovery path: as data centres reach full contracted revenue, infrastructure margin improves quickly.

But there are two holes in that commitment: ① FY2027 gross margin comes with direction only (down then up), no percentage; ② there is no timeline for when full contracted revenue arrives. How deep the crouch is and how long it lasts are both blank.

Analysts pushed on this. Guggenheim's John DiFucci asked about memory and SSD price increases eroding contract margins; management answered by distinguishing fixed-price contracts from cost pass-through mechanisms. Bernstein's Mark Moerdler asked about competitive threats from new entrants such as SpaceX; management sidestepped the margin implication and pivoted to customer retention and 97.5% GPU utilisation.

4. Core numbers and segment detail

Revenue of $19.18B (+21% YoY) beat the $19.09B consensus modestly; non-GAAP EPS of $2.11 beat the $1.96 consensus by 7.7%. But the EPS needs unpacking:

★ Non-GAAP EPS includes one-time investment gains (the Ampere chip business divestiture plus Bloom Energy warrants). The company itself discloses that excluding them, Q4 was $2.03 (still above consensus, beat narrowing to +3.6%) and full-year FY26 was $6.83 rather than the headline $7.63. Growth rates computed off the headline figure will be overstated.

The segment-level divergence is stark:

① OCI is the only story. $3.35B → $4.08B → $4.89B → $5.79B across four quarters, close to a straight line, +93% YoY.

② SaaS and legacy licensing have barely moved. Cloud Apps grew 10% YoY and just 2.5% QoQ; Software (licences plus support) fell 2% YoY. Oracle's legacy business is now a stable cash base, not a growth source — all growth rides on a single line, which makes concentration risk higher than the balance sheet suggests.

5. Guidance: raised across the board, with execution risk maxed out

The FY27 full-year revenue target of $90B is unchanged, non-GAAP EPS was raised to $8.05 (+18% excluding one-offs), and the long-term targets through FY2030 were reaffirmed at 31% revenue CAGR and 28% EPS CAGR.

Q1 FY27 revenue growth was raised from this quarter's actual +21% to +27–29%, a large step up. RPO conversion supplies the confidence; execution risk supplies the cost. Hitting it requires the data centres to be built on schedule, the GPUs installed and the customers onboarded. The disclosed progress at Abilene, Texas — 42% delivered, 35% due in the next 90 days — is what that pressure looks like in practice.

6. Valuation: 9.1 percentile — but does the same multiple as NVDA make sense?

📉 Forward PE 19.5x, the 9.1 percentile of its five-year range (five-year mean 34.6x, mean −1σ at 24.3x, now below that floor). Forward PS is 4.19x at the 29.4 percentile.

The gap between the two percentiles is itself informative: the PS percentile (29.4%) sits far above the PE percentile (9.1%), meaning what has been compressed is the earnings multiple, not the revenue multiple. The market isn't questioning whether Oracle can sell; it's questioning whether those sales become money for shareholders. That lines up exactly with falling gross margin, rising interest expense and equity dilution.

Cycle intensity (invest-playbook): the AI cloud capex cycle itself is still expanding (RPO $638B, 97.5% GPU utilisation, supply broadly behind demand). But the financing cycle has entered contraction — market tolerance for debt-funded AI capex fell noticeably between June and August 2026. With FCF already negative and heavy reliance on debt and equity issuance, Oracle is the most exposed name in this capex credibility crisis.

⚠️ One pricing worth questioning: ORCL's forward PE of 19.5x has fallen to the same level as NVDA's 19.85x. But their balance sheets are worlds apart — NVDA is a cash generator with no dilution pressure; ORCL has negative FCF and needs both debt and equity. Applying the same multiple to two such different balance sheets is questionable. That doesn't mean ORCL should be more expensive; more likely it means the market has yet to fully price the credit risk differential.

Analyst consensus: 31 analysts, 90.3% buy, average target $262.25 (high $400 / low $145). Against the 2026-08-03 price of $129.87 that implies enormous upside — but the $145–$400 dispersion in targets says on its own that analysts can no longer form a consensus on this company.

7. Peer group scan

Group definition: AI cloud infrastructure / hyperscale data-centre capex. The defining feature is building GPU data centres with enormous capital outlays and renting compute to enterprises and AI companies, with revenue and RPO expanding alongside training and inference demand — while bearing the triple pressure of capex intensity, free cash flow and debt-funded dilution. The group extends upstream to GPU suppliers and sideways to data-centre power and cooling.

Two observations:

① The group stratifies by who can afford it. MSFT, GOOGL and AMZN have core cash cows underneath, so capex pressure is diluted by business diversification; ORCL and CRWV have no such buffer, and the market gives the same RPO story a completely different valuation in their hands. A big RPO number cannot rescue a multiple on its own — the balance sheet has to hold.

② VRT is the one position in this group that bears no capital risk. It sells shovels to the people building data centres; whether or not their economics work, the equipment still gets bought. Forward PE 34.4x at the 31.8 percentile is not cheap, but the risk profile is categorically different.

9. Both sides of the argument

Three reasons to be constructive:

① The demand is real, not narrative. RPO of $638B (+363% YoY) is contracted; OCI's +93% is the fastest acceleration among the major clouds; GPU utilisation is 97.5%; four Q4 customers each signed above $8B; Multicloud AI Database grew 404% in the quarter. These are things that already happened, not projections.

② Customer prepay and BYOH is leverage management no peer has. $75B of hardware capital requirement is already covered by customer prepayments or customer-supplied GPUs, shifting part of the capital risk outward. That is genuine contractual differentiation.

③ Valuation is at the 9.1 percentile of five years, below mean −1σ. If FY27 guidance (+27–29% revenue, $8.05 EPS) is delivered, the growth implied by the current forward PE is understated — and 31 analysts are 90.3% buy.

Three reasons to be cautious:

① This is a capital-discipline problem, not just a spending problem. FY26 capex of $55.7B exceeded the company's own $50B guide, the second consecutive overrun, with roughly $70B more due in FY27. FCF deteriorated from −$0.4B to −$23.7B. Market trust in "capex converting into proportional returns" is draining, and trust takes a long time to rebuild.

② The financial structure is the biggest company-specific negative. Interest-bearing debt +$37B in a year to $129.5B; $40B of FY27 refinancing including $20B of dilutive equity; shareholder law firms investigating the capex disclosures. All three compound one another.

③ Gross margin has no floor, and growth is concentrated in one product line. Eight straight quarters down with management guiding to more but refusing to quantify; meanwhile Cloud Apps grew 10% and Software fell 2%. Everything rides on OCI. If OCI stalls there is no second engine.

★ The disagreement: both sides agree on demand; they disagree about the balance sheet. Bulls say "with a backlog that full, borrowing to build is rational." Bears say "borrowing to build is fine, but you couldn't hold to your own guide — why should I believe the next $70B?" Next quarter answers part of this (gross margin, revenue attainment); the full answer waits until the data centres are loaded, which is years away.
