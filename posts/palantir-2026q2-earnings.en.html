1. Seven straight quarters of acceleration — on a rising base

The usual shape for a high-growth name is "high but decaying" — the base rises, so YoY falls. Palantir's last seven quarters are the inverse: the denominator kept growing and the growth rate kept climbing. QoQ accelerated in parallel (13.6% → 16.0% → 18.6%), which rules out a single large contract inflating one quarter.

📌 The significance isn't the number itself — it's that it invalidates the default assumption baked into most valuation models: that growth decays on its own. Any DCF that ran "93% fading linearly to 40%" has been wrong for seven quarters straight. That doesn't mean it won't decay eventually. It means nothing in the data says decay has started.

2. Core numbers

Three worth pulling out:

① Rule of 40 = 155 (93% growth plus 62% FCF margin), an all-time high. Anything above 100 is already unusual; 155 essentially rejects the premise that growth and profitability trade off.

② Net retention of 157% (+700bps QoQ) means existing customers spend 1.57x what they did a year earlier. Combined with the large-deal counts (220 at $1M+, 98 at $5M+, 73 at $10M+, up from 47), growth is clearly shifting from new logos to existing-account expansion.

③ GAAP operating margin 47.1%, adjusted 62%. The CFO pre-announced that Q3 expenses rise meaningfully on seasonality — management actively damping expectations of linear margin expansion, with no equivalent language last quarter.

3. The bookings slowdown is an illusion — split it and see

All three bookings measures decelerated on a YoY basis this quarter:

On that table alone, the conclusion is "momentum is cooling." Split TCV by segment, though, and US commercial TCV went from +45% to +153% YoY — the deceleration is entirely the government base (large contracts signed a year ago). The core engine sped up.

📌 A reusable rule: when bookings growth decelerates, split by segment before drawing a conclusion. The total is a base-weighted average, and one segment's tough comp pushes it down in a way that looks identical to cooling demand. Palantir this quarter is the textbook case.

The rule cuts the other way too: if Q3 total TCV growth is still below this quarter's 49%, the base-effect explanation stops holding, and government demand needs re-examining (US federal budget and defence procurement cycles genuinely fluctuate).

4. US vs international: the biggest gap in the story

The international lines (26% / 42%) run far behind the US ones (149% / 90%), and the gap is not narrowing. Today's Palantir is a US AI champion, not yet a global one.

Management's own posture is the tell: they pre-announced on the call that international strategic commercial contracts will run under $500K per quarter — pushing their own international commercial expectations down to nearly nothing. The call summary contains no strategic explanation for the gap, which makes it the quarter's biggest narrative blank. No analyst asked either.

5. Guidance: three consecutive raises, +13.4% cumulative

Q3 2026 guide: revenue $2.160–2.164B (implying +11.7% QoQ, +83.1% YoY), adjusted operating income $1.292–1.296B. FY26 adjusted FCF was raised to $4.5–4.7B, and adjusted operating income from $4.44–4.452B to $4.889–4.897B.

Delivery: eight straight quarters of beating its own guide. Actual $1,935M against its own $1,801M high end, $134M (+7.4%) above. From the original FY26 midpoint of $7.19B to today's $8.154B, that's roughly +13.4% of cumulative raises in a year.

Note that the Q3 guide implies only +11.7% QoQ, versus +18.6% actual this quarter. On an eight-quarter track record it's most likely conservative — but if Q3 does land near +11.7%, that's the first genuine sign acceleration has ended.

7. The quality of the EPS beat: that $91.8M

GAAP diluted EPS of $0.41 against ~$0.34–0.35 expected is a 17–21% beat. But two things happened at once:

① GAAP EPS matched adjusted EPS exactly for the first time ($0.41 = $0.41). These two always had a gap before (SBC, tax adjustments); a sudden convergence is itself an anomaly.

② Other net income jumped to $91.8M from $68.2M last quarter. The company gave no explanation and no analyst asked.

📌 The logic to check: when GAAP and adjusted suddenly converge, there are two reasonable explanations — there genuinely are no adjusting items left (good), or both were lifted by the same non-core gain (bad, because adjusted is supposed to strip non-core items out). The available information doesn't distinguish between them.

The falsifiable check: if FY26 Q3's other net income (expense) comes in far below this quarter's $91.8M — say back under $20M — that confirms this quarter's GAAP EPS carried a one-time investment gain, and EPS growth drops sharply. The market currently isn't separating operating from non-operating contribution at all.

8. Valuation: a low percentile, but not a sell-off

Cycle read: Palantir's group — AI application-layer enterprise software — is still in a demand-validation phase, not maturity, and it isn't cyclical in the conventional sense. That means the anchor isn't a historical P/E band; it's the assumption about how long growth persists.

The 3.6th percentile is the easiest number to misread. It does not mean the price was crushed to a historical low — the price is near highs. It means trailing GAAP EPS grew far faster than the share price, enlarging the denominator. So this is "the multiple compressed because earnings caught up," which is a completely different situation from a sell-off.

Forward P/E is 86.1x and forward P/S is 37.8x. By conventional enterprise-software standards that remains expensive. Accepting it requires believing 93%-type growth persists for a meaningful stretch — and the seven-quarter acceleration record is the evidence for that, and the only evidence.

Analysts: 22 covering, average target $197.21 (high $255 / low $80), about +23% against the $159.9 price; 72.7% Buy / 18.2% Hold / 9.1% Sell. But those figures are from one day after the print, so most targets likely don't reflect this quarter yet. The revisions over the coming days are the meaningful signal.

9. Peer group scan

Group definition: AI application layer / enterprise AI software — firms that neither build frontier models nor sell compute, but package AI into decision, workflow or security products that enterprises consume directly, monetised through subscriptions and large contracts. Shared traits: multiples supported by growth rates, high gross margins, and extreme share-price sensitivity to whether AI is actually landing.

Three observations:

① Valuation percentiles inside the group are wildly bifurcated. PLTR sits at the 3.6th percentile on P/E (earnings catching up), while SNOW, CRWD and DDOG all sit at the 96th–100th percentile on P/S. Within one group, some names are at their historical cheapest and others at their historical richest — the market has stopped treating "AI software" as a single trade.

② What the laggards share is a story that hasn't reached the numbers. CRM has Agentforce, C3.ai has the enterprise-AI positioning, SOUN has voice AI — and their three-month returns are +1.4%, +4.7% and −30.0%. A narrative isn't enough; you need a 149% segment growth rate.

③ BBAI is the small-cap control for PLTR's government business: −25.6% over three months and still lossmaking. Government AI demand is real, but it clearly isn't spread evenly — PLTR is taking most of it.

11. Risks and the other side

1. The quality of the EPS beat is unclear. The source of $91.8M in other net income wasn't disclosed, and GAAP converged with adjusted for the first time. If it disappears next quarter, GAAP EPS growth falls sharply — and the market isn't separating the two today.

2. The evidence for international expansion is thin. 26% / 42% against 149% / 90%, a gap that isn't closing, with management itself pre-announcing international commercial at under $500K a quarter. Only the US half of the "global AI platform" story has been delivered.

3. Growth is shifting toward concentration risk. Total customer growth slowed from 31% to 24% YoY; growth is now driven by 157% NRR and large deals ($10M+ from 47 to 73). Top 20 customers already run $124M each on a TTM basis — if large-account renewals slow, the impact is far more concentrated than in a diversified base.

4. Government bookings decelerated. This quarter it's confirmed as a base effect, but another decline in Q3 turns US federal budget and defence procurement volatility into a real risk.

5. Positioning after a 26.8% single-day move. Well beyond the implied move, which leaves meaningful room for profit-taking.

The two sides: bears say "forward P/E of 86x is expensive on any arithmetic, and a single decelerating quarter compresses the multiple"; bulls reply "seven straight quarters of acceleration, eight straight beats of its own guide, and a Rule of 40 at 155 — judging that trajectory on a static P/E is the wrong tool." Neither is wrong. The disagreement is how many quarters of continued acceleration you're underwriting. Two quarters: today's price is fair. Eight: it's cheap. Zero: it's extremely expensive. This post can't settle that — it can only tell you nothing in the data yet shows the deceleration has begun.
