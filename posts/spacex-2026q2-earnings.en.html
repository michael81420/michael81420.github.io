SpaceX．Q2 2026 Earnings, Unpacked ｜🧾

SpaceX's first earnings report since its June 2026 IPO: revenue of $7.81B, up +92% YoY, well ahead of the $6.93B estimate; loss per share narrowed to −$0.09 (vs. −$0.26 expected).
But quarterly AI capex surged to $18.4B, eating nearly all operating cash flow, and the stock fell 8% in after-hours trading. Built from the IR release, call summaries, and Futu data. Not investment advice.

TL;DR · What actually mattered this quarter

🚀 A clean beat on the first report since IPO — but that's only half the story. Revenue of $7.81B, up +92% YoY (vs. $6.93B expected); loss per share of −$0.09, far better than the −$0.26 expected. All three business lines (Space, Connectivity, AI) grew.

💸 AI capex surged to $18.4B in a single quarter, rewriting the cash flow picture. Capex this quarter was $18.37B, with $15.83B going to the AI segment; the company does not disclose quarterly operating cash flow, but H1 operating cash flow of $3.47B minus H1 capex of $28.48B puts H1 free cash flow at roughly −$25.0B — deeply negative. This is the same script as Meta and Alphabet's AI capex anxiety this cycle — SpaceX is just running into it right at its public-market debut.

📊 AI is the fastest-growing segment, and also the biggest cash burner. AI segment revenue grew +247% YoY off a small base ($738M → $2.56B); Connectivity (Starlink) is now the largest revenue source at $4.29B (+66%); Space (the core launch business) is the smallest and slowest-growing segment (+29%).

★ The company has not yet established a formal financial guidance framework — only a verbal comment about reaching $100B in ARR by year-end. Forward PE is not meaningful given ongoing losses; trailing P/S is around 90x, a sign the market has already priced in a good chunk of the optimistic case.

1. Three segments: who's growing, who's burning cash

Connectivity (Starlink) is currently the largest revenue pillar, while AI has the smallest base but the most dramatic growth rate — meaning a large part of the +92% total revenue growth comes from the AI business expanding rapidly off a tiny base. That base effect will fade over time and shouldn't be extrapolated linearly into future quarters.

2. AI capex is eating cash flow — this is what the market actually cares about

Capex this quarter was $18.37B (up 82% QoQ from Q1's $10.11B), with $15.83B (86%) going to the AI segment — Connectivity got only $1.37B, and Space even less at $1.17B. This allocation shows capex's center of gravity has shifted away from rockets and satellites, toward AI compute infrastructure.

The cash flow statement only gives first-half figures: operating cash flow of $3.466B and capex of $28.476B, which puts H1 free cash flow at roughly −$25.0B. The company does not disclose Q2 operating cash flow on its own, so we do not estimate a single-quarter FCF; Q2 capex alone ($18.37B) is more than 5x H1 operating cash flow, so the direction is already clear.

📌 Falsifiable test: if capex keeps climbing at the next report (2026-11-05) without operating cash flow catching up, this isn't a one-time build-out peak — it's structural cash burn. Same logic used to judge Meta's AI capex story this cycle.

3. Guidance: no formal framework yet, just a verbal target

This is the first earnings report since the IPO, and the company has not given a formal revenue or EPS guidance range. CFO Bret Johnsen said on the call that the company is "on pace to reach $100B in annualized recurring revenue (ARR) by year-end" — a verbal, non-GAAP forward-looking statement, not formal guidance, and shouldn't be treated as the same kind of commitment as other companies' formal guidance ranges.

4. Valuation: forward PE not meaningful, P/S already rich

📉 Still loss-making, so forward PE isn't meaningful. At the 2026-09-25 stock price of $148.03 and a $2.07T market cap, trailing P/S (against $23.04B of TTM revenue) is around 90x — the richest implied valuation in the peer group besides AST SpaceMobile.

If management's verbal "$100B ARR by year-end" target is actually hit, forward P/S would compress to roughly 20x — but that's management's own optimistic scenario, not an analyst consensus, and the two shouldn't be conflated. The current rich valuation reflects that the market has already priced in a good part of that optimistic case.

5. Peer group scan

Group definition: commercial space / satellite internet. Covers launch services, low-earth-orbit (LEO) broadband, direct-to-device satellite connectivity, and (unique to SpaceX) an AI infrastructure side bet.

One cross-sectional note: SpaceX is the only company in this group running "launch + satellite + AI" all at once; most other players focus on just one or two of those. ASTS carries the richest valuation, reflecting optimism around the direct-to-phone satellite narrative; VSAT sits at the bottom, reflecting a stalled-growth turnaround story and standing as one of Starlink's most direct competitors.

7. Bull and bear cases

Three reasons to be bullish:

① A clean beat across the board on the first report, with all three segments growing. Both revenue and the narrower loss beat expectations by a wide margin, suggesting the pre-IPO financial disclosures weren't misleading.

② The loss is narrowing structurally, and adjusted EBITDA is strengthening sharply. Net loss narrowed from −$1.0B a year ago to −$541M, and adjusted EBITDA grew +191% YoY — the core earnings power is improving.

③ There's no immediate funding pressure. The IPO raised roughly $85.7B, the inaugural bond offering raised $25B, and cash plus marketable securities stood at $100B at quarter-end — enough to sustain the current capex pace for a while.

Three reasons to be bearish:

① Quarterly capex is already several times operating cash flow. $18.4B of quarterly spending far exceeds what operating cash flow can generate; if this pace continues, how fast the $100B cash pile burns down deserves close attention.

② Free cash flow has turned deeply negative, and the company is heavily reliant on capital markets. H1 FCF of roughly −$25.0B, per official first-half figures, shows the business is still far from self-funding.

③ The AI segment's high growth rate has an obvious base effect that will fade. +247% growth is built on a tiny $738M base a year ago and shouldn't be extrapolated linearly; what really matters is whether the company keeps landing new cloud services agreements (already $14.1B in contracted sales this quarter) to sustain the absolute scale.

★ The real disagreement: is this AI capex "building infrastructure ahead of the revenue" or "burning cash before the business model is proven"? Bulls point to the $14.1B in cloud contract sales and $47.5B backlog as demand-side evidence; bears point to the roughly −$25.0B H1 FCF and argue it's too early to call this a success.
